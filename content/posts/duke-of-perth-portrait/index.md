A tartan in a portrait is a tempting thing. It is **dated** — the painter fixes the cloth to a year
in a way a surviving fragment rarely is — and it is often the only witness to how a sett looked that
early. But it is also the hardest kind of evidence to read: the cloth is a flat painting of a
**folded, draped** garment, the grain bends over every fold, and two and a half centuries of varnish
have turned the colours brown. Our engine, which reads a sett cleanly from woven cloth, has no
purchase on a painting at all.

So we tried something deliberately strict. **Could we recover the sett from the painting alone —
without looking at the sitter's name, and without peeking at any tartan we already hold?** If the
cloth by itself leads us to the right answer, the method is worth trusting on portraits where there
*is* no known answer.

## Reading the cloth

**Undoing the varnish.** The white lace at the duke's throat ought to be white. In the painting it
reads as a muddy yellow-brown — proof of how much the old varnish has yellowed, and a ready-made
correction: neutralise the lace back to white, apply the same correction everywhere, and the true
hues reappear. (This is the same lesson as the [Tarim plaid reconstruction]({{< ref "TarimTartan" >}}):
age shifts colour in a *direction*, and a naïve read systematically under-counts the blues and
whites.)

**Reading the sett.** On the flattest, best-lit folds of the drape, the pattern resolves into:

- a broad **red** ground, by far the dominant colour;
- a **green** block set into the red;
- a narrow **blue** guard line where red meets green;
- and — the telling detail — a single fine **yellow** line beside the green.

That yellow line is the fingerprint. Plenty of Highland setts are red and green with a blue guard;
very few carry a thin yellow overcheck in just that place. From the cloth alone, our blind reading
came out as a reflective sett of red, green, blue and yellow — broad red ground, blue guard, green
block split by a fine line, mirrored.

## The match

Set that reading against the dictionary, and it falls squarely on one family:

### → [**Drummond of Perth**](/families/drummond-of-perth/)

The clan's hero sett, [Perthshire or Drummond of Perth](/setts/s9/r36w1db3ly1dg16r8db3t2w1~x2/), is a
broad red ground, a green block, blue guards, and the distinctive **yellow line by the green** that
our blind read picked out. It is the sett long associated with the dukedom, and said to have been
worn as a cloak by Prince Charles Edward himself.

We reached it from the wool, not from the man wearing it — and the man wearing it turns out to be the
**Duke of Perth**. The cloth and the sitter agree.

What the painting could *not* give us were the two palest threads — a fine **white** line and an
**azure** one — exactly the colours the varnish had buried. That is the honest limit of the method,
and a predictable one.

## Why it matters

Duprà painted this in **1739**. The earliest written record of the Drummond of Perth sett we hold is
James Logan's of **1831**, and the Register's documented dates run later still. Subject to
confirmation, this portrait is a **candidate earliest dated witness to Drummond of Perth — some
ninety years before the books**. A tartan in a painting, read carefully, does not just illustrate a
sett. It can *date* one.
